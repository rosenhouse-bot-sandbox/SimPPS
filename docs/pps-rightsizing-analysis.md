# Portland Public Schools "Rightsizing" Plan: Rationale, Process and How to Weigh In

*Compiled October 7, 2026, from Portland Public Schools (PPS) documents and local news coverage. The plan is still changing; check [pps.net/rightsizing](https://www.pps.net/rightsizing) for updates.*

## Summary

On Oct. 5, 2026, PPS released two school closure scenarios. Scenario A closes 14 schools and Scenario B closes 11; a third option changes nothing. Superintendent Kimberlee Armstrong's recommendation is due Nov. 17, and the school board is scheduled to vote Dec. 15. Approved changes would begin in fall 2027.

The district's case rests on enrollment and school size, not short-term savings. PPS has lost more than 6,000 students since 2018–19 without closing buildings, and it projects that by 2031–32 most of its elementary and middle schools will be too small to staff consistently. Public input has the most leverage before the mid-November recommendation.

"Rightsizing" is the district's term for the closure and reorganization process.

- **Seven K–8s become K–5s.** Their 6th–8th graders move to middle schools:
  - Beverly Cleary → Beaumont

  - Sunnyside Environmental → Hosford

  - Vernon → Harriet Tubman
  - César Chávez → George
- **MLC and Odyssey.** Metropolitan Learning Center's high school grades close, and its 9th–12th graders go to their neighborhood high schools. MLC becomes K–8. Odyssey moves into the MLC building, leaving its East Sylvan site empty for repurposing.
- **Sunnyside's focus option ends.** Sunnyside Environmental loses its environmental focus-option status. Current lottery students may stay through 5th grade, but no new lottery transfers would be accepted.

- **Roosevelt and Jefferson:** consolidating immersion programs, and middle school enrollment.

## 2. The district's rationale and the data behind it

### Fewer students, same buildings

- Enrollment fell by 6,086 students from 2018–19 (48,708) to 2025–26 (42,622). Over the same period, the number of school sites rose from 81 to 83.
- District projections show another 3,446 fewer students by 2031–32.
- Portland State University's Population Research Center, which produces PPS's annual enrollment forecast, projects 36,763 students by 2035–36, about 14% fewer than today. PPS did enroll about 150 more students than forecast in 2025–26, including about 200 more kindergartners.
- The center's director, Ethan Sharygin, says the main driver is smaller kindergarten classes, which stay small all the way through 12th grade. PPS cites a 30% drop in births in Portland over the past decade. Oregon's fertility rate fell from 58 to 45 births per 1,000 women over ten years.
- PPS is also enrolling a smaller share of the children who live in the district: 74% of kindergartners and 74.4% of students overall, down from 82.2% in 2015.

### A minimum size for a "sustainable" school

PPS set enrollment floors of 360 students for elementary schools, 500 for K–8 and middle schools, and 1,100 for high schools. The floors come from model schools in Oregon's Quality Education Model, a state formula for what schools need. The district says schools that size can consistently staff a librarian, art, PE, music and counseling.

- In 2025–26, 27% of elementary schools, 23% of K–8s and 40% of middle schools met the floor.
- With no changes, by 2031–32, 37 of 45 elementary schools and 13 of 15 middle schools would fall below it.
- In September, staff told the board that 15 elementary and 3 middle schools would need to close for the remaining buildings to be "sustainable."

### Cost per student

The Oct. 6 slide deck compares per-student costs for core staffing paid from the general fund, excluding special education, multilingual, Title I and Measure 98 money. The district average is $9,484 per student. Elementary schools average $10,037, middle schools $9,281 and high schools $8,610. Under the same staffing formula, Jefferson High costs about 70% more per student than Grant High ($13,638 vs. $8,010).

Small elementary schools cost more per student. The deck's examples:

### Not mainly about money, by the district's own account

- PPS projects a $65.2 million budget gap for 2027–28. That follows a $56.3 million gap for 2026–27 and roughly $170 million in cuts over the past several years.
- District spokesperson Valerie Feder told Willamette Week that PPS does not expect closures to produce "significant net savings in the short term," and that some operating savings would go toward helping students transition. In March, Armstrong said closing a school saves "not millions of dollars."
- Armstrong told OPB the goal is a standard level of service at every school, not closing the deficit, because the district is "spreading our resources too thin."
- None of the Oct. 5–6 documents estimates savings or transportation costs.
- Researchers interviewed by KATU and Willamette Week say closures rarely save much in the short term, mainly because staff move to other schools rather than leave; savings come later, if at all. Empty buildings keep costing money: PPS spent about $70,000 in one year on the long-closed Smith School. Board policy favors keeping surplus property in public hands, which limits sale revenue.

### How schools were picked

From the Oct. 6 slide deck:
- **Enrollment trends and capacity:** projected enrollment and capacity by grade band through 2031–32.
- **Facility and capital viability:** building condition considered alongside enrollment.
- **Program fit:** K–8 conversions, specialized programs, middle school class sizes, immersion pathways, and which schools feed into which.
- **Guardrails:** no closure sends students to more than two receiving schools, and students move into higher-quality buildings.

The memo adds that the criteria often conflict, so they were weighed against each other rather than ranked, with the balance varying by region. Armstrong also cited accessibility and mechanical systems, room for child care, and walkability. The scenarios project stability only through 2031–32, and more closures could follow after that.

### The guiding principles

The board adopted four principles on May 26 (Resolution 7307):
1. Sustainable, well-resourced schools with comparable programs at every grade level.
2. Safe and healthy learning environments, prioritizing walkability and safe routes.
3. A strong network of neighborhood schools, balanced against choice programs. Every school and program is part of the process.
4. Decisions guided by the PPS Equity Lens, centering Native and Black students, multilingual learners, students in special education, and students experiencing poverty.

### The equity argument and the pushback

**The district's case.** Small schools hurt high-poverty areas most. With no changes, the share of Title I middle schools large enough to meet the floor falls from 29% to zero by 2031–32, and Title I elementary schools from 18% to 9% (Sept. 15 board presentation). Under Scenario A, students in poverty would change schools at the same 27% rate as everyone else; multilingual learners would change at 31%. PPS's FAQ says more than 93% of families stayed in the district after past school changes. Armstrong has asked, "what happens if we do nothing?"

**The critics' case.**
- Both scenarios close the same six Title I schools: three in the Jefferson/Roosevelt area and three in Cleveland/Franklin, with 1,709 students between them. None of the Title I closures are in Grant/McDaniel or Wells/Lincoln. Title I schools make up 43% of closures under A and 55% under B (KATU analysis).
- The parent group Families for Equity in Schools argues that enrollment at several closing schools is low partly because neighborhood families transfer out: about 38% of Creston-area PPS students attend another PPS school, and nearly half of Woodmere's. It also argues that focus-option schools were spared.
- The Portland Association of Teachers wants a pause. Its statement says "closing schools is not a plan, it's a reaction" and asks the district to exhaust every other option first.
- Opponents at savepdxschools.org want the district to redraw boundaries and cut central office staff first.
- Board member Stephanie Engelsman warned that long bus rides would widen the equity gap.

### Lessons from the last rebalancing

PPS hired Dr. Terrance L. Green and Dr. Havala Hanson to audit its Southeast Portland enrollment and program balancing, which took effect in 2023. Their June 10, 2026 report drew on surveys of 905 families and 327 staff, records for about 52,000 students, and focus groups and interviews with about 100 people. It found:
- Students' test scores, attendance and discipline roughly matched comparable students at other schools, with small reading gains for some groups.
- Enrollment became more balanced, but 12 of the 20 affected schools fell short of projected enrollment.
- After the changes, 32% of staff left the affected schools, versus 19% elsewhere. Among staff of color, 39% left, versus 21%.
- The schools chosen served far more low-income students than the Southeast schools left out: 54% versus 25% eligible for free or reduced-price lunch.
- One school's conversion to all-Spanish immersion cut its share of Black students from 10% to 2%.
- Only 22% of families said they understood the changes at their school. Members of the advisory coalition felt their input was symbolic, and only 3% of staff surveyed trusted the district to handle rightsizing fairly.

The auditors recommended clear goals and a fixed scope from the start, plain and consistent communication, early engagement that genuinely shapes decisions, applying the equity lens to actual choices, visible follow-through, and public acknowledgment of past harms.

## 5. How public comment can shape the outcome

### Timing
The board votes on the superintendent's recommendation, so input before mid-November matters most. The memo says the recommendation will show how input shaped it. After Nov. 17, comment can still push the board to amend the recommendation or postpone.

### Direction
Expect the list to shrink or shift, not grow. Realistic outcomes:
- Scenario A, Scenario B, or a mix of the two
- different receiving schools, boundaries or program moves
- a delay

The three schools that close only under A are the clearest trade-off. Keeping them open leaves 43% of schools under the floor instead of 33%.

### Content
The criteria are spelled out, so facts about a specific school that address them give staff something to act on. Examples:
- projected enrollment
- the receiving school's capacity and condition
- travel distances
- immersion and special-education pathways
- how many neighborhood families already transfer out

### Accountability
- **Closure reports.** Board Policy 6.10.030-P, adopted in 2003, says the board may close a school only on the superintendent's recommendation accompanied by a School Closure Report that analyzes:
  - cost per student and projected overall cost or savings, including effects on other schools and transportation costs
  - projected enrollment for the next five years
  - the building: capacity, use, operating cost, ADA accessibility, physical condition, environmental concerns and capital needs
  - effects on equity of access, nearby schools, transportation and community partnerships, plus the history of transfers in and out
  - an implementation plan with a map of new boundaries

  The policy also requires a public process for reviewing closure recommendations with the community. In February, PPS said MLC's high school change didn't need such a report because it was a program change. Resolution 7307 doesn't mention the policy, so it's fair to ask whether each closing school will get a report before Dec. 15.
- **Promised analyses.** Resolution 7307 commits the district to be transparent about its data sources and to analyze, for each scenario, staffing, transportation, facilities, nutrition services, program continuity and finances. It also promises a standardized equity impact analysis that shows how community input shaped the recommendation.
- **The audit's own advice.** The district's equity audit faulted the last process for advisory input that changed little. It's reasonable to ask the district to show, at the Nov. 4 feedback summary and in the recommendation, what it heard and what it changed as a result.

### The votes
It takes four of seven. As of Sept. 1 (Willamette Week's account):
- Chair Eddie Wang, Michelle DePass and Virginia La Forte were most open to big changes.
- Patte Sullivan backed continuing.
- Vice Chair Rashelle Chase-Miller wanted more data first, and confirmed with Armstrong that the board could still stop the process.
- Christy Splitt preferred "a scalpel" over "a hammer." She said PPS should step back and compromise if families felt it was going too far, too fast, and she worried that larger schools in wealthier areas were being spared.
- Stephanie Engelsman wanted boundary changes tried first. On Oct. 6 she called the plan "mayhem."

  - Each speaker gets two minutes, once per meeting, in person or virtually. Slides and videos aren't allowed.

  - Comment on a voting item, such as the Dec. 15 vote, is taken during that agenda item.
